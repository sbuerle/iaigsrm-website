History and Evolution of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence emerged from centuries of developments in logic, mathematics, computation, neuroscience, engineering and philosophy. This chronology distinguishes intellectual precursors from the institutional field established in the mid-twentieth century. Dates mark publications or major milestones rather than claiming any single inventor created AI.

1 · Logic and mechanized reasoning, 1847–1930

1847–1854 · George Boole and algebraic logic

Boole formalized logical propositions using algebraic operations, laying groundwork for computational representations of reasoning. This was not AI itself, but an essential precursor to digital logic and symbolic inference.

Primary source: Boole, An Investigation of the Laws of Thought (1854).

1879–1910 · Frege, formal languages, and Principia Mathematica

Frege's predicate logic and Russell and Whitehead's formalization of mathematics helped establish the expressive languages later used in knowledge representation and automated theorem proving.

Primary sources: Frege, Begriffsschrift (1879); Whitehead & Russell, Principia Mathematica (1910–1913).

2 · Computability, neurons and cybernetics, 1931–1949

1931 · Kurt Gödel and incompleteness

Gödel demonstrated limitations of sufficiently expressive formal axiomatic systems. His work profoundly influenced mathematical logic and subsequent debates about machine reasoning, but does not itself establish that AI is impossible.

Primary source: Gödel, “Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme I,” Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik 38 (1931), 173–198. DOI.

1936 · Alan Turing and computable numbers

Turing introduced a mathematical model of effective computation, helping define what algorithms can calculate. Alongside Church's work, this supplied a foundation for computer science and computational AI.

Turing, “On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem” (1936–1937).

1943 · McCulloch and Pitts model artificial neurons

Their simplified mathematical neurons connected logic to networks of neural activity and became an early foundation for artificial neural network research.

McCulloch & Pitts, “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity” (1943).

1948 · Norbert Wiener and cybernetics

Cybernetics examined feedback, communication and control in animals and machines, providing concepts that influenced adaptive systems, robotics and control theory.

Wiener, Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine (MIT Press, 1948).

3 · AI becomes a field, 1950–1969

1950 · Turing's imitation game

Turing framed machine intelligence as a behavioral question, introducing the imitation game and anticipating objections about thinking machines.

Turing, “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” Mind (1950).

1955–1956 · Dartmouth and the naming of artificial intelligence

The 1955 proposal by McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon set out a research agenda for a 1956 summer workshop. This is a conventional founding moment for AI as an organized research field, not the beginning of all thinking about machine intelligence.

McCarthy et al., “A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence” (1955; reprinted 2006).

1956–1959 · Symbolic reasoning and early learning

Logic Theorist (Newell, Simon and Shaw) demonstrated heuristic theorem proving; Rosenblatt's perceptron work helped launch trainable pattern-recognition systems. Symbolic and connectionist approaches developed in parallel.

Rosenblatt, “The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain” (1958).

1966–1969 · ELIZA, robotics and early limitations

Weizenbaum's ELIZA illustrated natural-language interaction without human-like understanding. The Shakey robot integrated perception, planning and action; criticism of early perceptrons exposed limitations of particular network architectures.

Weizenbaum, “ELIZA” (1966); Minsky & Papert, Perceptrons (1969).

4 · Knowledge engineering and AI winters, 1970–1989

1970s · Logic programming and expert systems

PROLOG advanced declarative programming; DENDRAL and MYCIN demonstrated domain-specific knowledge representation and inference. These systems exposed challenges in eliciting, maintaining and validating expert knowledge.

Shortliffe, Computer-Based Medical Consultations: MYCIN (1976).

1970s–1980s · Funding cycles and commercial expert systems

Periods of reduced enthusiasm and funding—often called AI winters—followed overpromising, high costs and technical constraints. Commercial expert systems later expanded before encountering maintenance and scalability difficulties.

Secondary source: Nilsson, The Quest for Artificial Intelligence (Cambridge University Press, 2010).

1986 · Backpropagation popularized for multilayer networks

Research by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams helped establish efficient learning of distributed representations in multilayer neural networks.

Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, “Learning Representations by Back-propagating Errors,” Nature (1986).

5 · Statistical learning and reinforcement learning, 1990–2011

1990s · Statistical machine learning

Probabilistic graphical models, kernel methods and support vector machines strengthened data-driven approaches. Their success reflected both theory and improvements in available datasets and computing.

Cortes & Vapnik, “Support-Vector Networks” (1995).

1997 · Deep Blue defeats Garry Kasparov

IBM's chess system demonstrated high-performance search and evaluation in a well-defined domain, without implying general intelligence.

Campbell, Hoane & Hsu, “Deep Blue,” Artificial Intelligence 134 (2002), 57–83.

2006–2011 · Deep learning resurgence

Layer-wise pretraining, better optimization, graphics processors and large labeled datasets helped revive neural-network research and scale its applications.

Hinton, Osindero & Teh, “A Fast Learning Algorithm for Deep Belief Nets” (2006).

6 · Deep learning and foundation models, 2012–2019

2012 · AlexNet and ImageNet

A deep convolutional neural network achieved a major breakthrough in large-scale image classification, demonstrating the impact of GPUs, datasets and neural architectures.

Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton (2012).

2014–2016 · GANs and AlphaGo

Generative adversarial networks advanced generative modeling; AlphaGo combined deep networks with search and reinforcement learning to achieve a landmark Go victory.

Goodfellow et al., “Generative Adversarial Nets” (2014); Silver et al., “Mastering the Game of Go...” (2016).

2017 · Transformer architecture

Self-attention enabled a new approach to sequence modeling and became foundational for many later language and multimodal systems.

Vaswani et al., “Attention Is All You Need” (2017).

7 · Generative, multimodal and agentic AI, 2020–2026

2020 · Scaling language models

GPT-3 demonstrated strong few-shot performance at scale while also highlighting limitations in reliability, bias and evaluation.

Brown et al., “Language Models are Few-Shot Learners” (2020).

2021–2023 · Foundation models, instruction tuning and public adoption

The term “foundation model” helped describe adaptable pretrained systems; instruction tuning and conversational deployment broadened applications and sharpened debates over safety, transparency and governance.

Bommasani et al., “On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models” (2021); Ouyang et al., “Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback” (2022).

2023–2026 · International governance and assurance

Advanced AI prompted new voluntary frameworks, safety institutes, policy initiatives and legislation. Technical assessment, red teaming and model/system evaluations became central governance practices.

NIST AI Risk Management Framework; Japan AISI red teaming guide; Organizations and initiatives directory.

2024–2026 · Agentic systems and assurance challenges

Tool-using systems introduce questions about delegated authority, action monitoring, prompt injection, identity and access controls, and human oversight. Claims of AGI or superintelligence remain contested and should not be presented as established milestones.

OWASP GenAI Security Project; MITRE ATLAS.

Editorial and research policy

Primary papers are preferred when available. Later historical syntheses are clearly labeled. Dates, priority claims, translations and publication versions should be independently checked before publication. The chronology is selective, not exhaustive; planned expansions include Leibniz, Babbage, Lovelace, Shannon, Hebb, Samuel, LISP, SHRDLU, Japanese Fifth Generation Computer Systems, probabilistic AI, causal inference, robotics, and global research traditions.
