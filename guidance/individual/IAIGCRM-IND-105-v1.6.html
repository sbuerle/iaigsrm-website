Module 5 — AI Safety for Families, Children, and Older Adults

IAIGCRM-IND-105 · v1.6 12 October 2026

Learning outcomes: Recognize age- and circumstance-specific risks; establish an ongoing household AI safety culture; protect sensitive information; practice verification and emergency response; and complete a ten-point self-check.

1. Why this module matters

AI is now part of school assignments, games, messaging, voice assistants, shopping, medical-information searches and household administration. AI can be useful, but generated text, images and voices can also mislead. A household safety program must account for differences in experience, access, independence and exposure—not assume that every person of the same age faces the same risks.

2. Different needs, shared safeguards

AI tools are used by people of different ages, backgrounds and levels of experience. The risks are not identical for everyone: a child may encounter persuasive characters in a game, a student may face synthetic-image bullying, and an adult may receive a convincing financial scam. A useful household safety approach combines a few common rules with guidance suited to each person's activities, independence and needs.

Young children

Young children may not yet recognize when a friendly character, advertisement or chatbot is trying to influence them. They may also struggle to distinguish a real person from a simulated conversation. For example, an AI-powered game character might ask a child to share a photograph, name their school or continue chatting on another service.

Choose age-appropriate tools, supervise use when appropriate, and explain simple boundaries: never share personal details, photographs, passwords or location information without asking a trusted adult. Encourage children to report confusing or uncomfortable interactions without fear of punishment.

Teenagers and students

Teenagers often use AI for schoolwork, creative projects and social interaction. They may encounter manipulated photographs, synthetic voice recordings, impersonation, online harassment or AI companions that encourage secrecy. For example, a fabricated image shared in a class group chat could damage someone's reputation even when it is clearly false.

Discuss consent before creating or sharing images of others, verify sensational claims before reposting them, and identify trusted reporting channels at home and school. Agree on appropriate uses of AI for assignments, privacy settings and what to do when an interaction becomes threatening or emotionally uncomfortable.

Working-age adults and caregivers

Adults may use AI for work, banking, shopping, caregiving and household administration. Risks include fraudulent invoices, stolen login credentials, workplace-data exposure and overreliance on unverified medical, financial or legal advice. A realistic-sounding call from a supposed manager, for instance, could request an urgent payment to a new account.

Verify unusual requests through an established contact method, protect confidential workplace and family records, and check consequential AI advice against authoritative sources or qualified professionals. When caring for others, obtain consent before uploading their personal information or connecting an AI tool to their accounts.

Older adults and relatives living independently

Older adults have widely varying technical experience and should not be treated as a single group. Some may encounter impersonation scams, misleading technical-support alerts or accessibility barriers. For example, a caller using a cloned family member's voice may claim there is an emergency and demand immediate payment.

Encourage independent verification through saved telephone numbers, consider a prearranged family verification phrase, and make instructions accessible through clear language, larger text or device accessibility settings. Offer support with permission while respecting each person's autonomy and privacy.

Shared safeguards across the household

Different people may need different levels of supervision or assistance, but every household can benefit from a small set of consistent practices:

  • Verify unusual requests: End the conversation and contact the person or organization using a number or app you already trust.
  • Protect accounts: Use unique passwords, a password manager where appropriate, multifactor authentication and timely software updates. Never share one-time login codes.
  • Limit personal information: Share only what is necessary, and review privacy settings before uploading files, photographs or recordings.
  • Question convincing media: A familiar voice, realistic image or polished message does not prove that a claim is genuine.
  • Make help easy to obtain: Identify trusted contacts and explain how to pause, ask questions and report a problem.
  • Respect consent: Ask before accessing another person's messages, changing settings or sharing their data, except where immediate safety or legal obligations require action.

Household principle: Anyone should feel comfortable stopping an interaction, checking a claim and asking for help. Effective safeguards protect both security and personal dignity.

3. Building household digital literacy

Household AI safety is an ongoing culture, not a one-time conversation. New services, scams, school expectations and family circumstances change. Effective households build repeatable habits: discuss, practice, verify, report and improve.

Establish a Household AI Safety Charter

Write a short, visible agreement together. Include which AI tools are appropriate for which users; what information must never be entered; expectations for schoolwork and attribution; consent before uploading anyone's image or voice; how to verify urgent requests; and who can help. Invite input from children, teenagers and older relatives rather than imposing rules without explanation.

Make safety a routine

  • Weekly informal check-in: ask whether anyone encountered a strange message, AI-generated image or questionable answer. Discuss examples without blame.
  • Monthly ten-minute practice: rehearse a call-back, a suspicious login-code request or a fake delivery message. Rotate the scenarios.
  • Quarterly review: update permitted tools, device settings, account recovery methods, school rules and the household contact directory.
  • After any incident: support the affected person first; secure accounts, document what happened, report where appropriate and improve the process.

Model the behavior

Adults should visibly check sources, admit uncertainty and correct mistakes. Children should see that even confident AI answers can be wrong. Discuss how to distinguish generated claims from independently verified evidence. Avoid fear-based lectures and avoid making disclosure feel like a confession.

Suggested charter pledge: “In this household we protect each other's privacy, question urgent requests, verify important claims, ask for help early and treat mistakes as opportunities to improve.”

After a suspected breach or scam

  • Stop interacting with the suspicious message, app or caller; disconnect an affected integration if safe.
  • Use a trusted device and official website to reset the affected password and any reused passwords.
  • Enable or reconfigure multifactor authentication; revoke unfamiliar sessions and recovery methods.
  • Check account activity, forwarding rules, connected apps and payment settings for unauthorized changes.
  • Contact the bank, service provider or school promptly if money, personal records or a child’s account may be involved.
  • Restore important files from a verified clean backup if data was changed or deleted.
  • Preserve screenshots, dates and transaction identifiers, and report fraud to appropriate authorities.
  • Explain the incident calmly to household members, update shared rules and monitor for follow-on scams.

4. Consent, boundaries and trusted adults

Every member of a household has a right to privacy, including children, teenagers, adults and older relatives. AI tools can be helpful, but information entered into a service may be retained, processed or shared according to its settings and terms. A useful rule is to share only what is needed for the task—and to obtain permission before sharing information about someone else.

Ten types of information to protect

Before uploading text, images, audio or documents, check whether they contain any of these categories:

  1. Passwords and authentication: passwords, passkeys, one-time codes, recovery phrases and security-question answers.
  2. Government identification: Social Security numbers, passports, driver's licenses and photographs of identity documents.
  3. Financial information: bank accounts, payment cards, transaction records and account statements.
  4. Medical and health information: diagnoses, prescriptions, test results, insurance details and identifiable medical records.
  5. Children's information: names linked to schools, classrooms, bus routes, pickup arrangements or daily schedules.
  6. Addresses and whereabouts: home addresses, live locations, travel plans, commuting patterns and times a home may be unoccupied.
  7. Private images and recordings: family photographs, voice samples, videos, biometric details and intimate material.
  8. Education records: grades, student IDs, learning plans and confidential school communications.
  9. Employment and legal records: payroll, tax forms, contracts, client information and confidential workplace documents.
  10. Personal communications and account details: private messages, relationship disputes, account identifiers and device information that could help an impersonator.

Information can become identifying when details are combined. A photograph, school name and daily schedule may reveal more together than any one item does alone.

Consent and respectful boundaries

Ask before sharing. Do not upload another person's photograph, voice, messages or records without their permission. For children, use age-appropriate explanations and responsible adult oversight. Teenagers should be included in discussions about consent, reputation and the lasting effects of sharing. When helping an older relative, seek their agreement, preserve their independence and avoid taking control of accounts without authorization.

Protect information belonging to others. Family members may be willing to share a detail with you without agreeing to its use in an AI service. The same principle applies to classmates, coworkers, clients and friends.

How to use AI while sharing less

  • Start with a general question. Describe the situation without names, account numbers, exact addresses or identifying dates.
  • Redact before uploading. Remove identifiers, faces, signatures, barcodes, metadata and unrelated pages where feasible.
  • Use excerpts instead of full records. Share only the passage needed to answer the question.
  • Check service settings and permissions. Understand retention, sharing and connected-app access before entering sensitive material.
  • Choose a safer channel for sensitive tasks. Use the relevant official portal or a qualified professional rather than an unfamiliar chatbot when confidentiality matters.

If information is shared by mistake

Stop sharing additional material. Where possible, remove the uploaded content or revoke shared access, then review the service's privacy controls and connected permissions. If credentials or financial information were exposed, change affected credentials through official channels and contact the relevant provider. Preserve evidence and seek help if fraud, harassment or impersonation is suspected. Deleting a chat may not erase every retained copy, so do not assume deletion alone resolves the exposure.

5. Ten key risks and why they matter

  1. Child data exposure: Prompts and images may reveal school, location or identity information.
  2. Emotional overattachment: An AI companion may be mistaken for a trustworthy human relationship.
  3. Homework substitution: Uncritical AI use can weaken learning and misrepresent authorship.
  4. Synthetic bullying: Manipulated images or voices can be used to harass peers.
  5. Age-inappropriate content: A service may produce material unsuitable for a child.
  6. Family impersonation: Voice cloning can make emergency requests sound convincing.
  7. Financial scams: Fake support calls and investment claims can target any household member.
  8. Accessibility gaps: Poorly designed tools can confuse users with differing needs.
  9. Excessive monitoring: Overly intrusive controls can undermine trust and autonomy.
  10. Delayed disclosure: Fear or embarrassment may prevent a person from reporting a harmful interaction.

6. Practical actions for safer AI use

Choose age-appropriate AI tools and review their privacy, content and account controls. Explain that safety filters are imperfect. Practice responses rather than merely describing them.

Household practice scenarios

  • Login-code request: a message says “Read me the six-digit code to keep your account.” Do not share it; open the service independently and check account activity.
  • Relative in distress: a familiar-sounding voice urgently requests money or secrecy. Hang up and call the relative using a previously saved number; verify with another trusted person if necessary.
  • School or childcare change: a text claims pickup arrangements have changed. Confirm through the school's published office number or official parent portal.
  • Tech-support warning: a pop-up or AI voice says a device is infected and demands remote access. Close the prompt and contact a trusted support provider independently.
  • Synthetic bullying: a manipulated classmate photo appears in a group chat. Do not forward it; preserve minimal evidence and report through the school or platform's process.
  • AI health or financial answer: a chatbot recommends a medication change or investment. Identify the consequential claim, inspect its source and consult a qualified professional before acting.

Verification rule: For every consequential answer an AI generates, identify the claim that matters most. Ask what evidence supports it, then open the underlying source yourself.

Household rule: Everyone may pause and verify without embarrassment. A credible-sounding voice, image or AI answer never overrides this rule.

Household Security Contact Directory

Keep a printed copy in a known place and a protected digital copy. Assign a willing Household Security Point of Contact (“family Chief Security Officer”) to coordinate help, not to take ownership of others' accounts. Fill in the actual numbers locally; do not publish them in shared training materials.

  • Household Security Point of Contact — name, telephone and alternate
  • Emergency services — 911 in the United States for immediate danger; verify local emergency numbers when abroad
  • Local police non-emergency line — independently verified number
  • School administrator, counselor or safeguarding contact — official number
  • Bank or card fraud department — number on card or official website
  • Trusted relative, caregiver or neighbor — pre-agreed number
  • Local Adult Protective Services — state or county contact; for U.S. referrals see the Eldercare Locator at eldercare.acl.gov or call 1-800-677-1116
  • Healthcare provider or pharmacy — verified office number

For school use, encourage independent reasoning, citations and adherence to school AI-use policies. After a suspected incident, preserve relevant evidence, avoid redistributing harmful content and report to the appropriate platform, school, financial institution or authority.

7. Everyday examples

These ten examples progress from younger children to teenagers, adults and older relatives. Use them for brief household conversations or role-play.

  1. Young child — game companion: An AI character asks for the child's real name and school. The child declines and tells a trusted adult.
  2. Young child — family photograph: A photo-editing app requests a portrait. The family asks permission, checks settings and chooses not to upload identifiable images when unnecessary.
  3. School-age child — homework: The student asks AI to explain a method, checks the explanation and completes the assignment independently under school rules.
  4. School-age child — upsetting response: A chatbot produces frightening or inappropriate content. The child closes it and tells a trusted adult without fear of punishment.
  5. Teenager — manipulated image: A synthetic image of a classmate circulates. The teen does not share it and uses school or platform reporting channels.
  6. Teenager — secretive AI companion: A bot urges the teen to hide conversations. The teen discusses the interaction with a trusted adult and reconsiders the service.
  7. Adult — employer impersonation: A voice message requests an urgent payment or confidential file. The adult verifies through a known workplace channel.
  8. Adult — consequential AI advice: An AI tool makes a tax, legal or health claim. The adult checks primary sources and consults an appropriate professional.
  9. Older adult — grandchild emergency scam: A familiar-sounding caller requests money. The adult hangs up, calls the grandchild directly and seeks support if needed.
  10. Older adult — fake support alert: A pop-up demands remote access. The adult closes it and calls a trusted technical contact using a verified number, retaining control over their device.

8. End-of-module checklist

  • We teach young children never to share identifying details, photographs or schedules with unknown services.
  • We review age suitability and privacy settings for children's AI tools.
  • We discuss consent, synthetic media and bullying with school-age children and teenagers.
  • We encourage independent schoolwork and source verification.
  • We protect addresses, location, passwords, login codes, financial and health information for everyone.
  • We use independent call-backs for urgent money, identity or account requests.
  • We verify the most consequential claim in important AI answers against the underlying source.
  • We keep an up-to-date household security contact directory.
  • We allow anyone to report a mistake or suspicious interaction without embarrassment.
  • We revisit our Household AI Safety Charter and rehearse scenarios regularly.
